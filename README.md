Lunar South-Polar Candidate Regions and Mars-Analog Field Environments
Author: Manus AI
Date: 14 November 2026
Status: Preliminary assessment; this shortlist is not exhaustive.

Purpose and scope
This report compares three lunar south-polar candidate regions with three terrestrial Mars-analog settings, using only the evidence cited below. NASA’s October 28, 2024 announcement listed Malapert Massif, Mons Mouton Plateau, and de Gerlache Rim 2 among nine possible Artemis III landing regions, with no priority order 1. That dated list is not a final landing-site or permanent-base decision, and this report does not claim a current official selection. Lunar coordinates in the project dataset are map references, not exact site markers.

Lunar candidate regions
Malapert Massif
Malapert Massif is one of NASA’s nine unranked regions 1. Listing is not selection. South-polar ridges may receive extended sunlight useful for solar power, while permanently shadowed craters may preserve water ice 2. These are regional possibilities, not evidence of continuous light or accessible ice at a specific point in Malapert Massif.

Mons Mouton Plateau
Mons Mouton Plateau is also on the unranked list 1. NASA’s assessment factors include terrain, communication with Earth, illumination, science, and mission availability; landing sites will be chosen as launch dates approach 1. Its candidacy therefore does not establish local operating conditions or a base decision.

de Gerlache Rim 2
de Gerlache Rim 2 is another preliminary region, with no stated rank 1. The broader polar setting makes power, access, and resource investigation relevant topics, but regional descriptions do not confirm resources or uninterrupted sunlight at this candidate. All three regions remain preliminary long-duration study candidates, not selected permanent-base locations.

Shared environmental context
NASA says south-polar ridges may offer extended sunlight and permanently shadowed craters may preserve ice, but ice distribution is complex and does not guarantee extractable reserves 2. Slopes, darkness, and abrasive regolith also pose operational challenges 2. The LPI Lunar South Pole Atlas provides LOLA topography and slope data plus shadow maps 3. These support comparison, not proof of local continuous light, safe access, or recoverable water.

Terrestrial Mars-analog settings

Atacama / Yungay
Yungay’s hyperarid, low-organic soils provide a field setting for sampling and life-detection workflows. From 2016–2019, ARADS integrated drilling, rover, and science-instrument tests; Yungay was chosen for its environment and available facilities 4. Red Stone biosignatures were barely detectable even in the laboratory, underscoring detection limits 5. Atacama does not reproduce Mars’s atmosphere, gravity, or radiation.

Haughton-Mars Research Station, Devon Island
The station is based around Haughton impact crater in Devon Island’s cold, dry polar desert 6. Its science and exploration-technology work includes habitats, drills, rovers, communications, and crew human factors 6. This supports study of field operations and crew workflows. Official station coordinates are 75°26′N, 89°52′W 6. Haughton is a terrestrial analog, not an exact Mars replica.

McMurdo Dry Valleys
NASA includes the McMurdo Dry Valleys among Earth’s most Mars-like environments because of extremely cold and dry conditions 7. The comparison is limited to that broad characterization. The cited source gives no specific temperature values or individual experiment results, so none are asserted. How the analogs connect to exploration questions
The three field settings answer different questions. Yungay is relevant to integrated drilling, rover, instrument, and sampling workflows in low-organic soils, as well as the difficulty of detecting biosignatures 4. Haughton relates to surface operations such as habitat use, communications, mobility, and crew systems 6. McMurdo provides a broad cold-and-dry soil comparison 7. None directly tests lunar vacuum, reduced gravity, radiation, abrasive regolith, or polar illumination and shadow geometry. Their findings should not be transferred directly to lunar base-site selection. They can inform bounded questions about sampling, mobility, equipment integration, and crew operations; distinctly lunar conditions need separate analysis.

Uncertainty and next steps
This is a preliminary, non-exhaustive shortlist, not a ranking. NASA’s 2024 list does not decide an Artemis landing site or permanent base; it is unranked, and the announcement says mission availability and launch timing inform later selection 1. The sources discuss polar light and ice regionally, not candidate-specific illumination duration, ice quantity, or extractability 2. The LPI atlas can guide topographic and shadow review but is not operational validation 3.

A next desk-study step would compare all three lunar regions against the same factors: terrain, Earth communications, illumination and shadow, science goals, and mission timing. Local resource or light claims should remain uncertain until supported by site-specific evidence. Analog choice should follow the test question: Yungay for low-biomass sampling and integrated robotics, Haughton for field operations and crew systems, and McMurdo for cold-soil context. This assessment is neither an official selection nor an engineering certification.
